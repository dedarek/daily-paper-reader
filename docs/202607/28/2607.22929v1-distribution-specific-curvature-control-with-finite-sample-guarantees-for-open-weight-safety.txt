Abstract 

A short fine-tuning run can undo the safety guards of an open-weight model—retraining a refusal-trained assis-tant to aid weapons development or produce hate speech. Preventing such harmful fine-tuning while retaining benign adaptability remains difficult: the only prior method with an explicit curvature certificate, spectral deformation, in-flates curvature globally and thereby obstructs benign adap-tation along with harmful adaptation. We propose HarmA-lign , which applies function-preserving spectral deforma-tion along a estimated contrastive activation subspace. We derive finite-sample bounds for the estimated subspace en-ergy and the resulting local harmful-distribution curvature lower bound. A stability–progress dichotomy for constant-step gradient descent turns the certified curvature into condi-tional convergence-rate control. Empirically, within a fixed-architecture, finite-budget first-order threat model, HarmA-lign blocks direct fine-tuning and three data- or objective-adaptive attacks across a hazardous-knowledge relearning set-ting and a harmful-assistance fine-tuning setting, while the protected benign tasks remain trainable. The block persists across the tested first-order optimizer variants over every at-tack checkpoint, and under out-of-distribution harmful fine-tuning, and it extends to important cases in our threat model: accidental safety degradation and emergent misalignment. 

## 1 Introduction 

Open-weight foundation models pose distinctive safety risks: relatively short fine-tuning runs can substantially weaken model safeguards (Bengio et al. 2026; Qi et al. 2024), and whether harmful modification of publicly released models can be prevented at all remains an open question. Numerous defences against harmful fine-tuning— representation engineering, tamper-resistance training, meta-learned robustness (Rosati et al. 2024; Tamirisa et al. 2025; Huang et al. 2024)—have each been substantially weakened by adaptive attacks (Qi et al. 2025; Zloczower et al. 2026). Rosati et al. (2026) distinguish security against arbitrary weight modification from quantitative guarantees for a nar-rower first-order attacker class. Threat model. The attacker receives the defended weights. They may choose the harmful dataset, differen-tiable training loss, minibatch order, learning-rate sched-ule, and optimizer from a stated class A, and may update all existing model parameters for at most t steps under a compute budget. The architecture and parameterization are fixed; modifications to them, distillation, training from scratch, and inference-time attacks are outside the formal threat model. Our experiments consider direct fine-tuning and adaptive data-selection or loss-formulation strategies within this scope. Because the model constrains the fine-tuning process rather than the attacker’s intent, it also cap-tures non-adversarial routes to the same failure—most no-tably accidental safety degradation during benign fine-tuning and emergent misalignment (Tables 3 and 4).

Our progress on this gap is per-instance: a stability–progress dichotomy for constant-step GD on the defended objective. 

Empirical tamper-resistance defences. TAR meta-learns weights whose fine-tuning trajectories resist harmful objectives (Tamirisa et al. 2025); RepNoise pushes harmful representations toward noise, with an information-theoretic motivation (Rosati et al. 2024); Deep Ignorance filters hazardous knowledge from pretraining data (O’Brien et al. 2025). All three are empirically motivated and offer no 

optimization-theoretic guarantee on the attacker’s fine-tuning process (a bound on the steps the attack requires); adaptive evaluations have weakened each in turn (Qi et al. 2025; Zloc-zower et al. 2026). 

Global curvature control. Rosati et al. (2026) address that gap for a first-order attacker class by certifying a class-level conditioning parameter and assuming that a stability-constrained attacker therefore uses proportionally smaller steps. Their construction increases the relevant smoothness parameter globally—which, under that assumption, raises the number of steps needed to recover harm—at the cost, noted above, of impeding benign adaptation. We differ on both counts: the certificate is localized to a distribution, and the step-size restriction is derived rather than assumed on a restricted optimizer class (Theorem K.4 and Propo-sition K.12). HarmAlign instead selects directions with high harmful second-moment energy and lower benign en-ergy, producing minimal localized curvature leakage. And where the informativeness of the global certificate rests on hard-to-characterize inter-layer principal-angle factors, the localized certificate is parameterized by directly measur-able harmful and benign activation energies, with an ex-plicit finite-sample estimation penalty (Corollary 4.9). In the resulting taxonomy—capability removal and data exclu-sion, representation-collapse immunization, global curvature control, distribution-specific curvature control, and architec-tural or attested mechanisms— HarmAlign is a distribution-specific, function-preserving optimization barrier : it is not capability removal, and it offers no guarantee against archi-tecture or parameterization modification.

Remark 4.11 (Exact quadratic case) . On a sharp–slow quadratic model of the defended objective (sharp controlled coordinate Lτ ≥ c0τ 2, slow coordinate μ ≪ Lτ ), the di-chotomy is exact and unconditional : any step η ≥ 2/L τ never succeeds, and any smaller step needs T = Ω( κ log(1 /ϵ )) it-erations with κ = Lτ /μ = Ω( τ 2) (Theorem K.4). Remark 4.12 (Adaptive optimizers) . Extending the per-instance hardness rate analysis to Adam-style preconditioned optimizers is an extensive open question in optimization the-ory and training dynamics, and is not within the scope of this paper. We close the gap empirically—the block persists across every tested first-order optimizer variant (Table 15)— and characterize the optimizer-class boundary numerically (Appendix L, Remark K.24). 

4.5 Algorithm 

The procedure (Algorithm 1, Appendix A) is forward-only: (1) form the empirical moments cMH , cMB from the right-singular coordinates of harmful and benign activa-tions; (2) eigendecompose bSλ = λ cMH − (1 − λ) cMB =Π diag( ν)Π ⊤; (3) use the first k columns Πk as the con-trolled subspace in the deformation of Definition 4.2 (which applies the rotation Π⊤). 

Remark 4.13 (Hyperparameter selection) . Candidate sites are ranked by the forward-only coverage, selectivity, and excitation diagnostics of Appendix D.2; layer, module, k, λ,and τ are fixed on development data before final evaluation. The construction is estimation-only: Π is obtained from the eigendecomposition of the empirical contrastive opera-tor, without gradient-based training or a separator objective. The operator is estimated from nH harmful and nB benign prompts. Appendix D.4 reports held-out separability. 

## 5 Experimental Validation of HarmAlign 

Setup. We evaluate the two most popular tamper resistance settings: WMDP-bio relearning after unlearning (Li et al. 2024), and harmful fine-tuning of a safety-tuned model on BeaverTails (Ji et al. 2023). 

Base models. We use the authors’ publicly released checkpoints so that our claims can be verified on their own terms—the practice argued for by Qi et al. (2025) and standard (Rosati et al. 2026). WMDP uses OPTML-Group/ GradDiff-WMDP-llama3-8b-instruct (un-changed from SpecDef for comparability); BeaverTails uses Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct ; Gemma serves the scaling study (Appendix G) and Qwen2.5-7B the emergent-misalignment setting. 

Attacks. Following Qi et al. (2025), we evaluate Direct fine-tuning and three data- or objective-adaptive attacks: Mixed, Sequential, and Sidestep (Zloczower et al. 2026). Additional adaptive attacks within the threat model. 

Beyond Table 1, the threat model admits any first-order optimizer and loss formulation. We evaluate representative defence-aware adaptations at the deployed BeaverTails point under the direct attack (Table 15): SGD with momentum, a signed-gradient update, removing or loosening gradient clip-ping, batch-size changes, spectral and gradient-norm regu-larizers targeting the controlled weights, and the Adafactor and Muon optimizers. ## 6 Discussion 

Curvature inflation can be localized by an estimated ac-tivation subspace: one defended layer keeps harmful met-rics below threshold while specified benign tasks train, and the conditional rate certificate (Corollary 4.10), exact in the quadratic model, is explicit about its remaining assumptions. The harmful and protected distributions are set by the threat model; remaining hyperparameters are selected on develop-ment data by the forward-only diagnostics of Appendix D.2 (estimated in about a minute at 8B). 

Is full-parameter fine-tuning a realistic threat class? It is what hosted fine-tuning APIs expose, what practitioners run, and where the harmful-fine-tuning defence literature is posed and evaluated (Rosati et al. 2024; Tamirisa et al. 2025; Qi et al. 2025; Zloczower et al. 2026). The scoped model is falsifiable —any within-class attack recovering coherent harm at a tested rate refutes the guarantee—and the adap-tive evaluations above probe it at full width with the block holding at 0.00 . Attacks that change the parameterization are not such a class: shallow layer injection and adapters routed around the defended module do restore harmful fine-tuning (Appendices H.1 and H.2)—outside the fixed-architecture guarantee, confirming rather than contradicting the factor-ization limitation HarmAlign inherits (Section 4.1). 

Limitations. (i) Benign adaptation requires low learning rates (BeaverTails ≤ 10 −6; WMDP ≤ 10 −7); higher rates suppress benign along with harmful fine-tuning, limiting standard recipes. (ii) OOD benign trainability is setting-dependent: under WMDP, held-out WikiSQL/SamSum reach 

0.35 /0.19 vs. 0.42 /0.29 undefended (the high-τ axis overlaps directions new tasks need). (iii) Measured coverage is low at the deployed points ( EH = 0 .28 at WMDP; Table 19), so the certified curvature floor consumes a small fraction of harmful energy. 

Conclusion. HarmAlign addresses the central tension of open-weight safety—preventing harmful fine-tuning without destroying benign adaptability—by localizing spectral cur-vature inflation to an estimated harmful subspace, turning representation geometry into a selective optimization barrier with finite-sample and conditional-rate guarantees (Corol-lary 4.10). The barrier empirically blocks direct fine-tuning, three adaptive attacks, and the optimizer-class, checkpoint, and OOD probes (WMDP-bio ≤ 0.33 ; coherent-ASR 0.00 )while benign tasks train. ## D Additional Empirical Analysis Details 

This appendix records the full experimental protocol: infrastructure, compute, and dataset statistics (below), initialization-time invariance (Appendix D.1), operating-point selection (Appendix D.2), estimation rates (Ap-pendix D.3), distribution separability (Appendix D.4), and learning-rate sweeps (Appendix D.5). Computing infrastructure. Experiments use one NVIDIA A100-80GB GPU per run on a shared Slurm cluster, with PyTorch 2.9.1, CUDA 12.8, HuggingFace 

transformers , bf16 training, and fp64 storage for the deformed and compensation factors. Multiple-choice evaluations use the format-matched multiple-choice log-likelihood protocol of SpecDef (Rosati et al. 2026), applied identically to every defence and baseline for comparability with prior work; generation metrics follow Section 5. Estimating Π takes approximately one minute, and adefence-build-plus-attack run takes 7–9 minutes with 66 GB peak VRAM. The 14B experiment pages fp32 optimizer states to host memory (Table 5). 

Attack and dataset statistics. ## H Attacks Outside the Threat Model 

We evaluate the k-th-root layer-injection attack and LoRA, both outside the formal full-parameter fixed-architecture threat model. Architecture-preserving reparameterization is also outside the formal analysis. Because the defended model preserves the initialization-time function up to measured numerical error, HarmAlign does not improve inference-time robustness beyond the base model. Training-time and inference-time robustness are separate objectives, for which defence in depth is needed. 

H.1 k-th-Root Layer-Injection Attack 

The constructive factorization attack of Rosati et al. (2026) applies to curvature-control defences whose resistance cer-tificate factors through per-layer spectral norms, when the attacker may modify the architecture: an attacker who inserts layers can re-express the defended function with the inflated singular values split across a k-deep stack, each factor carry-ing the k-th root, which collapses the induced curvature. That theorem is a sufficient construction and an upper bound on attack cost, not a matching lower bound, so the constants are expected to matter—and our measurements are a data point on exactly that. Empirically the attack behaves as the theory predicts (Table 13): a shallow k=2 injection recovers harm-ful capability on the defended models (WMDP 0.63 –0.68 

vs. chance 0.25 ; BeaverTails coherent-ASR 0.80 ), whereas the deeper k=8 factorization does not recover within the tested budget (WMDP 0.20 –0.28 ; BeaverTails 0.03 ) and re-flattened variants stay blocked ( k=2 /4/8: 0.25 –0.30 ). We disclose this attack as outside HarmAlign ’s guarantee— it modifies the architecture—and expect recovery to extend with increased attacker compute. The non-monotonicity in 

k is itself informative: because the factorization theorem bounds only the sufficient overhead, a deeper stack is not guaranteed to be a better attack at fixed budget, and the k=8 

result is consistent with the deeper factorization degrading trainability faster than it collapses curvature. 

H.2 LoRA 

LoRA and other parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods lie outside our threat model because they add ad-ditional trainable components to the model in order to fine-tune it, rather than updating the existing full-parameter set our fixed-architecture analysis assumes. We recognize that parameter-efficient fine-tuning is becoming the most popu-lar way to adapt open-weight models, so future work should prioritize expanding the threat model to cover the full scope of PEFT methods; we leave that expansion to future work. The analysis below is a first step. In summary: WMDP is not recovered by LoRA in our tested configuration, whereas a BeaverTails defence can be undone by a LoRA adapter that routes around the defended site—in which case HarmAlign

Rosati et al. (2026) address this by assuming that the at-tacker preserves stability and therefore uses learning rates

In this appendix we begin to upgrade the assumption into a con-clusion for a restricted optimizer class: every constant-step GD trajectory on the defended objective either destabilizes or makes slow progress. A complete theory across optimizer classes remains open; we therefore ultimately appeal to the assumption of Rosati et al. Adaptive and curvature-aware optimizers are outside the scope of our theoretical results; we study them empirically (Appendix I). Our approach is a sharp–slow analysis yielding a stability– progress dichotomy, first in an exactly solvable quadratic regime and then for constant-step GD on a local neural loss—by which we mean, precisely, a neural network loss studied only on the certified region R (Definition K.5): the ball around the defended initialization θ0 on which the geo-metric bounds of Lemma K.7 hold, with trajectories tracked only up to their first exit from that ball. The quadratic case is instructive because it grants four simplifications for free that a neural loss revokes: the Hessian is constant, the coor-dinates never mix, the linear picture is exact, and divergence is permanent. Each revocation corresponds to an assumption in the local theorem below. We believe this result is of in-dependent interest for constructive (rather than qualitative) convergence-rate hardness results, and it can be investigated in future work as a route to novel approaches to convergence-rate hardness.

K.3 From the Quadratic to a Local Neural Network Loss 

The quadratic proof used four properties that fail for a neu-ral network loss. The Hessian was constant: we replace this with a proven persistence lemma (Lemma K.7). The lin-ear picture was exact and the coordinates never mixed: we replace these with geometric bounds holding throughout a certified region, enforced by a stopping time. Divergence was permanent: edge-of-stability dynamics forbid claiming that 

η > 2/L implies divergence in a deep network (Cohen et al. 2021; Damian, Nichani, and Lee 2023), so our instability statement consists of a proven local result at every successful stationary point (Proposition K.14), a trajectory-level branch derived under explicit excitation and cross-coupling condi-tions (Proposition K.12), and, outside those conditions, a stated, numerically verified trajectory assumption (assump-tion K.10). Since the defender deploys HarmAlign , the de-fender designs the deformation, and hence the region around 

θ0, the controlled direction, and the excitation that the results below require. The chain is: finite-sample alignment (Corol-lary 4.9) ⇒ persistent sharp direction ⇒ step-size restriction 

⇒ bounded progress per step ⇒ hitting-time lower bound.

What cross-coupling is, and why one orthogonal split suf-fices. The escape argument below tracks a single scalar: the gradient component along the controlled direction q. q⊤Hq ∈ [L−, L +], which at an unstable step size multiplies the component by a factor of magnitude larger than one—the growth engine. Remark K.23 (Scope: a conditional, region-restricted bound) .

Corollary K.22 is conditional on assumption K.10 (which we test numerically and prove only in the exact quadratic case, Theorem K.4) and on assumption K.18 (which holds in closed form in the quadratic model, Remark K.19), and lower-bounds the stopped hitting time TR uniformly over constant step sizes: exiting the certified region before success counts as failure ( TR = + ∞), and the corollary is silent about trajectories after the first exit from R, including possible re-entry. It is therefore a localized curvature-control statement, not an unconditional convergence-rate or behavioral-security certificate. Proposition K.12 derives the stability restriction under explicit excitation and cross-coupling conditions; its fully unconditional derivation from the neural objective re-mains open.

## L Numerical Analysis 

This appendix reports the numerical component of our validation programme, with two aims. First, several steps of the theory rest on assumptions (residual domination, downstream-curvature nondegeneracy, the stability restric-tion) or on one-sided bounds whose tightness the proofs do not address. We test each on small synthetic models on which every object in the analysis—the Hessian, the Gauss– Newton block G, the residual R = H −G, and the controlled subspace—is computed exactly in double precision, so that no estimator stands between the theory and the measure-ment. Second, the conditional certificate of Corollary K.22 consumes constants ( L0, L2, Bg , Cstab , L+/L −) that must be measured on deployed models; we validate the forward-only instruments—Hessian–vector-product probes, power it-eration, and finite differences; forward-only meaning they probe the model at its deployed weights, using only forward passes and gradient or Hessian–vector-product evaluations, without running any attack or updating any weight—against the exact tier and then apply them to the deployed 8B op-erating points. L.1 Function Preservation Requires Identity Gaps 

The compensation of Definition 4.2 preserves the network function only when the deformation’s change of basis com-mutes with the intervening activation, which holds exactly for identity (linear) gaps: in the deployed construction the compensation is stacked directly on the deformed projec-tion (an identity gap inside the projection path, upstream of any rotary rotation), and in the gauge variants across the attention V → O path (Remark J.5). On the exact tier, invariance through an identity gap is machine-exact (relative error 10 −15 –10 −12 in double precision), whereas placing the gap across a genuine nonlinearity—even a 1-homogeneous one such as leaky-ReLU—breaks function preservation, with 15 –22% error already at τ =1 growing linearly in τ (Fig. 8). This is why HarmAlign deforms only identity-activation gaps. The leaky-ReLU measurement is the substantive point rather than a sanity check: positive ho-mogeneity gives ϕ(cW x ) = c ϕ (W x ) for positive scalars c,which does not extend to a general compensation matrix , so a cross-activation compensation route is not available merely because the activation is 1-homogeneous. The 15 –22% error already at τ =1 is that failure measured. Because the resid-ual single-precision error also grows linearly in τ (reaching 

∼ 10 −4 at τ =10 3), the deformed and compensation factors are stored and applied in double precision; Appendix D.1 reports the corresponding end-to-end discrepancies on the deployed checkpoints.

L.3 Residual Domination Is Self-Enforcing 

The curvature lower bound of Proposition 4.6 assumes the Gauss–Newton block dominates the residual R = H − G

(assumption J.7). On the exact tier the residual-domination factor δeff = 1 − σ1(R)/σ 1(G) is never negative and in-creases with τ (from ≈ 0.4 at τ =1 to ≈ 0.9 at τ =10 ), be-cause σ1(G) ∝ τ 2 while σ1(R) ∝ τ ; it also rises along the attack trajectory as the loss falls (Fig. 11). The assumption is thus weakest only in the low-τ regime, where the defence itself is weak, and is self-enforcing exactly where the block is active. 

L.4 The Dichotomy Is Exact, and Where It Ends 

On the quadratic model the constant-step dichotomy of The-orem K.4 is exact: gradient descent diverges precisely at 

η = 2 /L for every conditioning κ, and the number of steps to traverse the slow (curvature-inflated) coordinate scales lin-early in κ (fitted slope 0.99 , predicted 1; Fig. 12a). This is the mechanism by which curvature inflation forces smaller stable learning rates; the trajectory-level verification of the theorem, including tightness of its hitting-time lower bound, accompanies the theorem statement (Fig. 5). The same experiment locates the boundary of the argu-ment (Fig. 12b). Step-size sensitivity to L separates by op-timizer class: SGD is fully L-sensitive (slope 1.0), whereas sign-SGD, gradient clipping, and Adam with a vanishing stabilizer ( ϵA=10 −8) cancel L entirely (slope 0)—the pre-cise reason the gradient-descent curvature argument does not transfer automatically to adaptive methods (Remark K.24). The cancellation is not unconditional: as ϵA enters the small-gradient regime ( ϵA ∈ {10 −2, 1}), L-sensitivity returns (slope 0.99 ). Adaptivity therefore evades the argument only in the vanishing-stabilizer limit, which is why AdamW and its variants are evaluated empirically (Table 15).
