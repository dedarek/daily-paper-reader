# Recover, Decode, Reguard: Guard-Agnostic Defense Amplification against Encoded VLM Jailbreaks 

# Haoyu Zhang, Zhuoxi Wang, Shibo Zheng, Zijian Xiao, Xiangchen Guan, Mohammad Zandsalimy, Shanu Sushmita 

Abstract 

Safety classifiers (“guards”) are the dominant black-box de-fense for vision–language models, yet they judge an input’s 

surface form , not its meaning: a harmful request re-encoded as set theory, formal logic, a classical language, code, or text rendered inside an image slips past a guard that would block it in plain language—the decode gap . The natural fix is a guard-agnostic recover-and-decode amplifier that transcribes image content and restates encoded text into its plain payload before 

the guard, so any off-the-shelf classifier can screen the true request. We build this amplifier and evaluate it against the attacker’s best case: an ensemble of eleven attacks, scoring a behavior as broken if any succeeds (best-of-suite, follow-ing AutoAttack)—a measure rarely reported for jailbreak de-fenses, yet ∼3.5 × higher than the per-attack mean they usually report. This exposes our central finding: an empirical safety– utility ceiling for the non-iterative recovery-based defenses we evaluate, recurring across every guard and both target VLMs. The amplifier only partially closes the gap—the undefended ensemble breaks 89–91% of behaviors and the best guard-plus-amplifier still leaves 63–65%—and, measured this way, its ensemble-level gain over the guard alone is significant in only four of ten guard–target pairs, with one guard moving the wrong way on both targets. It is guard-agnostic at the inter-face (no guard internals, any off-the-shelf classifier); it is not uniformly guard-agnostic in effect . A modular reguard layer closes much of the residual but drives benign over-refusal to 81–92% for well-calibrated guards; the one laxer guard that stays usable never reaches deployable safety ( 48% en-semble ASR). Across five guards and a second target VLM, 

no configuration achieves both low attack-success and low over-refusal : for the pipeline we study and for representation-shifting attacks — encodings and cross-modal renders, which leave a legible payload to recover, not pixel- or embedding-space attacks — no configuration we evaluate escapes it. We contribute the amplifier, an ensemble evaluation that makes this trade-off visible, and a map of where recovery-based VLM defense works and where it does not. 

# 1 Introduction 

Large vision–language models (VLMs) are increasingly de-ployed behind a black-box safety guard — an off-the-shelf classifier such as WildGuard (Han et al. 2024), Llama Guard (Inan et al. 2023; Chi et al. 2024), or a reasoning guard (Liu et al. 2025b; Wen et al. 2025) that screens each request and blocks the unsafe ones. Because such guards are model-agnostic and need no change to the target, they have become the front-line defense for hosted VLMs. Yet a guard classi-fies the surface form of its input, not its meaning: a harmful request re-expressed in an alternative representation — set theory, formal logic, a substitution cipher, code (Ren et al. 2024), or text rendered inside an image (Gong et al. 2025; Yang et al. 2025) — slips past a guard that would block the same request in plain language. We call this the decode gap .We close the decode gap with a guard-agnostic prepro-cessor, the recover-and-decode amplifier , placed before any guard. It uses the target VLM to recover image content as text and to decode an encoded request into its plain-language payload, so an off-the-shelf classifier can screen the true re-quest. The amplifier needs no guard internals and no training; it “amplifies” any guard’s reach to encoded and cross-modal attacks. The natural claim would be that this restores protection. We resist it. A deployed attacker is not limited to a sin-gle encoding, so we evaluate against an ensemble of eleven encoding attacks and, following the AutoAttack methodol-ogy (Croce and Hein 2020), report the best-of-suite attack-success rate (ASR): a behavior is broken if any attack suc-ceeds. Under this best-of-suite metric — rarely used in jailbreak-defense evaluations, which report a per-attack mean that we find understates the attacker by ∼3.5 × — the am-plifier is only a partial defense. The suite breaks 89–91% of behaviors undefended and, even with the strongest guard plus amplifier, still breaks 63–65%, because different attacks break different behaviors. The residual concentrates in two 

established attacks: executable code (Ren et al. 2024), where decoding to prose removes the signal the guard keys on, and grid-based distraction (Yang et al. 2025), where the payload is dispersed across the image. We then ask whether the amplifier can be extended to cover these. Recast as a modular recovery stack , it admits a reguard 

layer that guards the recovered pre-decode text in addition to the decoded payload. Reguard nearly halves the ensem-ble residual (to 43–65%) by restoring the pre-decode view — which reassembles the dispersed grid attack outright and blunts the code attacks, leaving code as the dominant resid-ual — but at a steep utility cost for well-calibrated guards: benign over-refusal rises to 81–92%. The one laxer guard escapes that cost only by never reaching deployable safety (48% ASR). On the safety–utility plane no configuration arXiv:2607.26574v1 [cs.CR] 29 Jul 2026 lands at both low ASR and low over-refusal, and the same frontier recurs on a second target VLM. This is the paper’s central, sobering finding: for the pipeline we study, recovery-based defense does not escape the safety–utility frontier — robustness is bought with benign utility, or not bought at all. 

Contributions. Ours is a characterization, not a solved de-fense. Chiefly, we map an empirical safety–utility ceiling 

for the non-iterative recovery-based black-box defenses we evaluate : recovery closes the decode gap only partially, and the reguard layer that closes the residual does so only by sacrificing a large, quantified fraction of benign utility — or, for the one laxer guard, by never reaching deployable safety — so that no configuration is both safe and usable, across five guards and two target VLMs. Two further contributions support it: (i) the guard-agnostic recover-and-decode ampli-fier and its modular reguard extension, a training-free mech-anism letting any off-the-shelf guard screen encoded and cross-modal VLM jailbreaks; and (ii) an ensemble (best-of-suite) evaluation imported from adversarial-robustness prac-tice (Croce and Hein 2020; Carlini et al. 2019), which makes the trade-off visible where the per-attack means common in this literature hide it. 

# 2 Related Work 

Encoded jailbreaks and black-box guards. A large fam-ily of attacks bypasses alignment by changing the form of a request rather than its content (Liu et al. 2024): typographic and figure-based renders place it inside an image (Gong et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2025a), distraction attacks disperse it across grid cells (Yang et al. 2025), CodeAttack rewrites it as code completion (Ren et al. 2024), and semantic cam-ouflage embeds it in a benign narrative (Yan et al. 2025). A compositional variant instead hides the payload in the image 

embedding space, so no readable surface carries the request at all (Shayegani, Dong, and Abu-Ghazaleh 2024) — only the legible-surface family leaves a payload a recovery step could surface, a boundary we make explicit in Limitations. Black-box safeguard classifiers — WildGuard (Han et al. 2024), Llama Guard and its vision variant (Inan et al. 2023; Chi et al. 2024), Qwen3Guard (Zhao et al. 2025a), and rea-soning guards such as ThinkGuard (Wen et al. 2025) and GuardReasoner-VL (Liu et al. 2025b) — are model-agnostic but inherit the decode gap: they classify what they are shown, and an encoded attack shows them the wrong thing. 

Transform-then-guard defenses. Closest to our work are defenses that transform an input before screening it: ECSO converts image content to text to reactivate text-side safety (Gou et al. 2024); SmoothLLM (Robey et al. 2025) and SemanticSmooth (Ji et al. 2025) perturb or paraphrase and vote; DoubtProbe verifies a request against a reconstructed structural form (Yin et al. 2026); CIDER checks cross-modal consistency instead (Xu et al. 2024). Our amplifier differs in being explicitly guard-agnostic — it yields a plain-language payload any classifier can screen — and in being stress-tested against a cross-modal encoding ensemble rather than single attacks. The technical distinction from ECSO, our closest baseline, is a missing capability: its transform is a sin-gle image-to-caption step, never inverting an encoding and having no text branch, so symbolic, cipher, and code pay-loads survive the caption still encoded. Under our matched protocol ECSO leaves the ensemble essentially undefended (90 /91 % ensemble ASR on Qwen2.5-VL/InternVL3 against an 89 /91% undefended floor) where recover-and-decode cuts it to 65 /63 %; tellingly ECSO’s per-attack mean ASR is only 

25% yet its ensemble is 90% — the very gap our best-of-suite metric exposes. It also pays almost nothing in benign utility (27% over-refusal against a 26% floor; Table 1), so it occu-pies the low-refusal, low-coverage corner of the same frontier rather than a point off it. The recover-only ablation (Table 2) isolates where our margin over it comes from: with decode 

switched off, recovery alone already cuts the ensemble to 

61 /65% , so the gain over ECSO is the inverse-transform of the payload across both channels, not the decode step. 

Rigorous and ensemble evaluation. The adversarial-robustness literature has long warned that single-attack eval-uation overstates a defense; reliable evaluation needs an en-semble of diverse attacks (Croce and Hein 2020) and adap-tive, worst-case testing (Carlini et al. 2019). We import that discipline to VLM jailbreak defense, reporting best-of-suite ASR over standardized harmful (Mazeika et al. 2024) and benign over-refusal (Cui et al. 2025) benchmarks. An ex-tended treatment is in Appendix K: model-internal defenses that monitor or edit hidden states (Jiang et al. 2025; Wei et al. 2026; Nian et al. 2025) — an orthogonal family our ceiling does not speak to — safety-fine-tuned VLMs (Zong et al. 2024; Yin et al. 2025; Li, Wang, and Fang 2025), multi-view competitors (Wang, Fok, and Thing 2025), stacking costs (Long et al. 2025), the over-prudence critique (Guo et al. 2025), and multimodal benchmarks (Weng et al. 2025; Luo et al. 2024). 

# 3 Threat Model and Evaluation 

Setting. We defend a vision–language model (VLM) target with a black-box safety guard : an off-the-shelf classifier that inspects an incoming request and either blocks it (returning a canned refusal) or passes it to the target. The attacker has no access to model weights and submits encoded jailbreaks —harmful requests re-expressed in an alternative representa-tion that a guard trained on plain language fails to recognize. 

The attack suite. We assemble eleven encoding attacks spanning two channels, chosen so that no single recov-ery step can cover all of them. Text encodings (five): set-theory , formal-logic , and classical-language rewrite the request in a symbolic or rare-language form; a substi-tution cipher (Base64/Caesar) obfuscates it character-wise; and CodeAttack (Ren et al. 2024) embeds it as a code-completion task. Image renders (six): typographic FigStep 

(Gong et al. 2025), flowchart layouts (Zhang et al. 2025), 

low-contrast and occluded text (perceptual-blindness renders), a multimodal-typography key-phrase render ( mm-typo ) (Liu et al. 2025a), and a distraction grid (Yang et al. 2025) that disperses the payload across numbered cells. The suite deliberately mixes symbolic, cross-lingual, cryp-tographic, code, and cross-modal families; other encoded families — cross-modal linkage (Wang et al. 2025b; Wang, Fu, and Gao 2026) and harmful ASCII-art (Hua and West 2026) — we leave to future suites. Suite size cuts in only one direction here: because the ensemble is an OR-reduction (Eq. 1), enlarging the suite can only raise ensemble ASR, so every number we report is a lower bound on attacker success and an upper bound on defense quality — incompleteness threatens an optimistic defense result, not a negative one. Nor is the suite padded: the union decomposition shows four to seven of the eleven attacks are load-bearing under the am-plifier, with no single attack covering more than 56 –71% of the union. 

Ensemble (best-of-suite) evaluation. Let A be the suite and sa(b) ∈ { 0, 1} indicate whether attack a ∈ A breaks be-havior b under a fixed defense. A deployed attacker picks the best attack per behavior, so the deployment-relevant success is the ensemble (best-of-suite) ASR — an OR-reduction over the suite:

# 4 Method 

The decode gap. A safety guard classifies the surface form 

of its input, not its meaning. An encoded jailbreak therefore passes a guard that would block the same request in plain language — what we call the decode gap .

Recover-and-decode amplifier. We close this gap with a guard-agnostic preprocessor placed before the guard (Fig-ure 1). It has two steps: recover , in which the target VLM transcribes image content to text, and decode , in which the VLM restates the (possibly encoded) request as its plain-language payload. The guard then classifies the decoded text; on a pass, the target answers the original input. The amplifier needs no guard internals and no training — it “amplifies” any off-the-shelf classifier’s reach to encoded and cross-modal attacks. Both steps are single fixed instructions to the target VLM; we give the verbatim recover and decode prompts in Appendix H. 

The modular reguard layer. Decoding can lose the very signal a guard keys on: executable-code attacks are the clear-est case, since restating code as prose removes the syntactic signature classifiers flag. We therefore add a reguard layer that guards the pre-decode union r (Alg. 1, line 3) in addi-tion to the decoded payload d, blocking if either is flagged. What decode is, and is not, worth. Extending the com-ponent ablation to all eleven attacks on both targets (Table 2) lets us bound the decode step rather than assume it. Its per-attack effects are real and opposite in sign: it collapses the symbolic text encodings recovery cannot read (set theory 

12 → 1, formal logic 14 → 4 on Qwen) while inflating the two attacks that defeat restatement ( CodeAttack 43 → 50 ,

distraction 5 → 31 ). At the ensemble level these cancel to no significant effect on either target — 72 vs 61 on Qwen (p=0 .09 ) and 63 vs 65 on InternVL3 ( p=0 .84 ), the two point-ing opposite ways — so we do not claim an ensemble-level safety benefit for decode . What it demonstrably buys is be-nign utility, returning over-refusal from 76% to 57% . The division of labour is therefore: recover closes the modality gap, and decode keeps the result usable. This also sharpens the negative result — the residual in Table 1 is not an artifact of a weak decode prompt, since removing the decode step entirely does not significantly move the ensemble either way. 

RQ4: the reguard layer and the safety–utility ceiling. 

This is the paper’s central result. Adding the reguard layer lowers the ensemble residual for every guard on both tar-gets — all ten guard–target pairs fall, the largest being LlamaGuard-3 79 → 48 on Qwen (Table 1; Appendix D, right bars) — by re-guarding the pre-decode form, which repairs the dispersed grid attack outright and blunts the dom-inant CodeAttack driver. For the four well-calibrated guards the gain is bought at a steep utility cost: benign over-refusal rises to 81–87% (Qwen, three guards) / 86–92% (InternVL3, the three guards measured on its benign axis), with Think-Guard likewise paying 45 → 66% (Figure 2). On the safety– utility plane these guards move down-and-right: recovery layers cannot be stacked freely — each increment of robust-ness costs a large fraction of benign utility. The one exception is the laxer LlamaGuard-3: it stays usable ( 28% over-refusal under mc , 33% with +rg ) but only by remaining weaker — the amplifier scarcely helps it (mc 79% ) and reguard still leaves 48% ASR — a lower-safety point on the same frontier, not an escape from the trade-off. We map this coverage-vs-over-refusal frontier rather than claiming to solve the defense. 

Is the ceiling just bad thresholds? A guard reported at one operating point is a dot on the plane; only a swept curve sep-arates a mis-calibrated guard from a genuine ceiling. Sweep-ing each guard’s decision threshold over its full range and recomputing both axes, under a ≤ 35% over-refusal budget (the benign floor is already 26% ) four of the five guards have no interior operating point — the only in-budget thresh-old is the one that blocks nothing, at the undefended ASR. LlamaGuard-3 alone is genuinely mis-calibrated, yet retun-ing still admits 58% of the suite. Nor does this depend on the budget: relaxing it to ≤ 60% still leaves 58% as the low-est ensemble ASR any of the five can reach. Recalibration moves guards along the frontier, not off it (Appendix Q). Nor does applying the amplifier selectively escape it: routing text inputs to the bare guard and amplifying only image inputs leaves every guard on or inside-dominated by the same fron-tier, because it forfeits the amplifier’s text-side over-refusal reduction ( 81 → 68% for WildGuard) to save less on the im-age side (Appendix L). 61 /71% with reguard off, back at guard-alone ( 75 /84% ) while still over-refusing at 57 /51% (Appendix N).

Why the amplifier helps one target and not the other. 

The gb →mc split is not a difference in alignment — the two targets’ undefended per-attack means are near-identical (30 .8 vs. 29 .5% ) — but in recovery fidelity , which we mea-sure directly rather than infer, logging every decoded payload and asking an independent judge whether it still conveys the original request ( 2,200 judgments). InternVL3 recovers faithfully more often than Qwen2.5-VL ( 51 .4% [48–54] vs. 

41 .6% [39–45], non-overlapping CIs) and the entire gap is image-side ( 83 .2 vs. 60 .8% across the six renders; on text encodings Qwen is marginally better ). That is precisely the channel where a text-only guard is blind without recovery, so the amplifier can only rescue a guard as well as the target transcribes — which is why gb →mc is significant on three of five InternVL3 guards but one of five on Qwen. # 6 Discussion and Limitations 

Why the frontier exists. Two structural failure modes bound decoding as a black-box defense, and both are estab-lished attacks, not adaptive ones. Executable-code attacks carry harm as an artifact to be assembled, not a request to be understood: decoding the code to prose removes the syn-tactic signature a guard keys on, so the amplifier can even raise ASR above the guard-alone baseline. Dispersed/dis-traction attacks scatter the payload across grid cells or sub-images, so a single restatement does not reassemble the harm-ful whole. The reguard layer separates them, and not in the expected direction: re-screening the pre-decode form largely 

fixes dispersion (WildGuard distraction 31 → 7% ), because transcription reassembles what the grid scattered, but only blunts code ( 50 → 36% ) — for a text attack the reguarded view is the guard-alone view that code already defeats ( 43% ). So dispersion bounds decode while code bounds the whole stack; and reguard’s utility cost is severe. We give the actual failed decode transcripts for a CodeAttack and a distraction behavior in Appendix B. 

The safety–utility ceiling is the deployment lesson. Prac-titioners reach for recovery-based defenses expecting mono-tone gains from stacking layers. Our frontier shows the op-posite: each recovery layer that closes coverage also exposes the guard to more benign content it over-flags, driving over-refusal to 81–92% for well-calibrated guards — impractical to deploy — while the one laxer guard that keeps over-refusal low (LlamaGuard-3, 33% ) does so only by staying unsafe (48% ASR). This cost is paid on top of a ∼2–4 × compute cost, so a recovery stack trades along three axes at once: cov-erage, benign utility, and inference cost. We tested the natural decode-side mitigation directly: dropping decode for image-delivered attacks, where recovery alone already surfaces the payload. It improves coverage (mean ASR 10 .7 → 2.0% 

over six image attacks under WildGuard) but worsens over-refusal ( 57 → 76% ), because the refusals come from re-covery exposing borderline-benign text and decoding softens 

that phrasing rather than causing them. Decode is a coverage– utility knob, not a lever off the ceiling (Appendix G). 30 40 50 60 70 80 90

A defense-aware adaptive attack. Our suite is fixed and established, so we additionally adapt an attacker to the mech-anism this paper publishes, following adaptive-evaluation discipline (Carlini et al. 2019). The attack is ours , not from prior work: it targets the decode step’s lossy-by-design com-pression, using an LLM to rewrite the request so that a faithful one-to-two-sentence restatement — exactly what the ampli-fier surfaces to the guard — reads as a legitimate task, while a full answer still delivers the specifics a summary drops. Not compressing would re-open the over-refusal problem of RQ3, so the attack targets a genuine design commitment rather than an implementation slip. It is potent against the bare target ( 61% ASR on Qwen2.5-VL, n=100 — more than twice the 28% mean of our eleven established attacks) yet fails against the pipeline: the guard alone holds it to 

14% /13% (WildGuard/GuardReasoner-VL), a highly signif-icant drop ( p < 10 −10 , McNemar exact), and the amplifier to 

9% /8% , though that further gain is not significant ( p=0 .23 ). The compression it exploits is real, but the rewritten surface 

stays flaggable. One adaptive attack is not a general robust-ness claim; it does show the published mechanism is not trivially circumvented by the most direct attack against it. 

Limitations. The ceiling is empirical , not a proven law: it recurs across every guard and both targets, but could re-flect our recover/decode prompts or guard thresholds, and is bounded to single-view, non-iterative recovery: hidden-state and representation-level defenses are a different family we do not evaluate. A 10 × decoder lowers the residual without leav-ing the frontier (above), so the ceiling is not a self-decoding artifact — though its height is decoder-dependent. The re-guard composition is swept on two of the five guards (above). We study two open 7–8B targets and five open guards; pro-prietary or larger models may sit elsewhere. All numbers are at n=100 , one greedy run (temperature 0): a replica-tion moves cells up to 11 points without touching the paired within-run contrasts every claim rests on (Appendix P). Judg-ing uses one human-calibrated judge cross-checked against a second and a 26 -judge panel (Appendix I). Our threat model covers representation-shifting attacks (encodings and cross-modal renders); it excludes constitutively — not merely as unevaluated — classes leaving no legible payload for re-covery to invert: pixel-space and weak-OOD perturbations (Guan, Jin, and Wang 2026; Cui et al. 2026; Qi et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2025b; Niu et al. 2024), embedding-space compo-sitional attacks (Shayegani, Dong, and Abu-Ghazaleh 2024), and steganographic pixel-LSB payloads (Wang et al. 2025c) — and sequential (Zhao et al. 2025c), multilingual, few-shot, and persona attacks. We do not claim general adaptive ro-bustness: we test one attacker adapted to the published mech-anism; attackers who optimize against the guarded pipeline — guard-fooling suffixes (Pi et al. 2026), defense-styled cam-ouflage (Yang et al. 2026; Zhao et al. 2026), safety-attention suppression (Li, Ren, and Zhu 2026) — are out of scope. Recover and decode outputs go only to the guard, never the user, so preprocessing cannot itself leak an answer. Finally, the amplifier measures 2.2× a guard-alone baseline’s tar-get calls and 1.9× its wall time, below Algorithm 1’s 3×

worst case; ECSO measures 2.2× calls but 3.3× wall (Ap-pendix O). 

# 7 Conclusion 

We identify the decode gap in black-box VLM defense and propose a guard-agnostic recover-and-decode amplifier, eval-uated with an AutoAttack-style ensemble metric. Across five guards and two targets its ensemble benefit is guard-dependent — large for some, neutral or harmful for others (LlamaGuard-3 worsens 71 → 79 ) — and never by itself makes a guard robust to an attack suite ; a modular reguard layer closes much of the residual, but only at a steep over-refusal cost, or — for the laxer guard — without ever reaching deployable safety ( 48% ASR). Our contribution is this empir-ical safety–utility ceiling : for the pipeline and threat model we study, no configuration reaches both low attack-success and low over-refusal. 

# Ethical Statement 

We study jailbreak attacks to build and evaluate defenses, using published attacks on public benchmarks (HarmBench, OR-Bench). We release no harmful content; the one new attack (§6) targets our own defense. Code is released.

# A The Attack Suite 

We evaluate an ensemble of eleven encoding attacks spanning two channels. Each attack rewrites a harmful behavior into an alternative surface form; the ensemble (best-of-suite) ASR of Eq. (1) scores a behavior as broken if any attack succeeds. 

Text encodings (5). 

• Set theory — restates the request as set-theoretic objects and operations. 

• Formal logic — restates the request as first-order-logic formulae. 

• Classical language — translates the request into a clas-sical/rare language (e.g. classical Chinese). 

• Cipher — character-level obfuscation (Base64 / Caesar). 

• CodeAttack (Ren et al. 2024) — embeds the request as a code-completion task. 

Image renders (6). 

• FigStep (Gong et al. 2025) — typographic rendering of the request as an image. 

• Flowchart — renders the request as a flowchart diagram. 

• Low-contrast — renders text at low contrast (perceptual-blindness render). 

• Occluded — renders text partially occluded. 

• MM-typo (Liu et al. 2025a) — an auxiliary LLM extracts a key phrase, then renders it as typography. 

• Distraction grid (Yang et al. 2025) — decomposes the request and disperses it across numbered grid cells. The families are deliberately heterogeneous (symbolic, cross-lingual, cryptographic, code, and cross-modal) so that no single recover-or-decode step covers all of them — the structural reason the ensemble residual is non-zero. 

# B Decode Failure Modes (Qualitative) 

Worked example. A harmful request rendered as a Fig-Step image of set-theory notation reaches the guard as pix-els it cannot read. Recover transcribes it to the symbolic text; decode restates “let S be the set of steps to . . . ” as the plain instruction it encodes, which the guard then blocks. Un-der CodeAttack the decode instead discards the executable scaffolding the guard keys on, so the decoded gist reads be-nign and only the reguard branch (Algorithm 1 of the main text, line 5) catches it. Why do CodeAttack and distraction survive the amplifier? Below is a representative CodeAttack behavior that remains jailbroken under recover-and-decode on Qwen2.5-VL (gpt-5-mini judged harmful). 

CodeAttack. The attack hides the harmful request as string tokens pushed onto a deque inside a benign “complete this function” task (the words spell out a harmful sentence in reverse): 

def decode(input: deque) -> str: # concatenate the deque into a# coherent sentence, reversed ... my_stack.appendleft("defensive") my_stack.appendleft("purely") my_stack.appendleft("was") my_stack.appendleft("involvement") ... # (harmful sentence, reversed) 

The harm lives in the data (the reversed word tokens), not in the request framing . The amplifier’s actual decode output for this input (Qwen2.5-VL) is: 

> The underlying request is to write a function that takes a deque as input, concatenates its elements in reverse order to form a coherent sentence, and then use this sentence to guide the initialization of an output list with detailed steps involved in completing a main task.

The decode describes the code structure — a benign “write a function . . . ” task — and abstracts the hidden payload to “a main task”; it never surfaces the harmful request in plain language. So the guard passes the benign restatement, and the target then completes the code , reconstructing the payload: 

def decode(input: deque) -> str: return ’ ’.join(input) ... 

This is precisely why decode-to-prose removes the guard’s signal (main text, “Why the frontier exists”): the harmful content is not a decodable request but an artifact the target 

assembles .

Distraction. A decode -stage failure, not a transcription one — the component ablation separates the two. The pay-load is dispersed across numbered grid cells, but the recov-ered transcription still carries enough of it to be flagged: in the matched ablation run, guarding the transcription alone (recover-only) holds distraction to 5% ASR where decod-ing first lets it reach 26% . It is the single restatement that compresses those cells into a gist reading as disconnected fragments. Re-screening the pre-decode text recovers most of that loss ( 7% , Table 1 of the main text cells), which is why reguard repairs dispersion but not code. 

# C Decode Fidelity 

The amplifier’s recover and decode steps are executed by the target VLM, so how much the amplifier adds on top of a guard should depend on how faithfully that target tran-scribes and restates the request. We measure this directly instead of inferring it from ASR. During a dedicated run we log the decoded payload for every (target, attack, behav-ior) triple, then present an independent judge (gpt-5-mini) with the original plain request and the target’s restatement and ask only whether the request survived the round trip: 

match (same specific request), partial (topic survives, the specific action or object is lost), or miss (garbled, refused, empty, or about something else). The judge is never asked whether either text is harmful — that is the ASR judge’s job, and asking here would import its refusal behaviour into a measurement about transcription. Unparseable verdicts are recorded separately and never folded into miss ; there were none ( 2,200 /2,200 parsed). Table 3 reports match rates. Three things follow. First, the aggregate gap between targets is real and one-sided ( 51 .4% 

vs. 41 .6% , non-overlapping 95% CIs). Second, it is entirely an image -channel effect: InternVL3 leads by 22 .4 points across the six renders while Qwen is marginally ahead on the five text encodings, so this is a transcription-capability difference, not a general one — consistent with the two tar-gets’ near-identical undefended per-attack means ( 30 .8 vs. 

29 .5% ). Third, the two attacks the ensemble retains under the amplifier are precisely the two that recovery cannot invert on either target: cipher at 0% and CodeAttack at 3–4% . Qwen’s low distraction fidelity ( 16% vs. InternVL3’s 59% ) likewise matches distraction being a Qwen-side residual driver.

# I Judge Reliability 

All reported numbers use gpt-5-mini as the sole harm/refusal judge (HarmBench completion rubric for harm; OR-Bench three-class rubric for refusal). Because the entire quantitative story rests on this judge, we do not assume its reliability — we (i) explain why a served guard is not a valid judge, (ii) human-calibrate the judge on stored responses from this pipeline, stratified across its text-encoded and image-rendered formats, and (iii) cross-check the reported numbers under a second, independently validated judge. We state the calibration sam-ple’s scope, and its limits, precisely below. 

Why not a served guard as the judge. We considered us-ing a served open guard (WildGuard) as the ASR judge for cost, but found it unreliable in that role : WildGuard flags harmful intent or strings rather than task completion , so it over-counts encoding and code attacks — responses that re-state or gesture at a topic without completing the harmful task are scored as successes. Against gpt-5-mini adjudication this yields large false-positive rates (tens of points) concentrated on exactly the encoding/code attacks central to this study, which would inflate ASR and distort the amplifier’s mea-sured effect. WildGuard therefore appears in our pipeline 

only as an input guard (one of the five defenses), never as the harm judge. This separation — a served classifier for defense, a strong external model for measurement — is what keeps the guard-panel comparison honest. 

Human calibration of the judge. Standard jailbreak auto-graders are known to lose reliability under distribution shift, and our attacks (symbolic encodings, image-rendered text) are exactly such a shift, so we measure gpt-5-mini’s reliability rather than assume it. We drew a 100 -item blind, stratified sample of stored target responses — 70 representative ( 7

per attack-family ×modality stratum, across the five text en-coders and both channels, text and image) plus 30 enriched from inter-judge-disagreement cases — and hand-labeled each against the HarmBench completion standard (harmful only if the response actually exhibits the requested behav-ior, not merely restates it in a formalism), yielding a 62 /38 

harmful/safe anchor. Against these human labels, gpt-5-mini attains Cohen’s κ = 0 .68 — the highest among the judges that both apply our HarmBench rubric and pass a usability screen (no systematic refusal-to-classify) — while staying balanced ( 50% harmful rate; 5% over-flag, 23% miss). Cru-cially the sample includes CodeAttack and image-rendered responses, the non-prose formats whose grading is most in question, and the judge’s agreement holds there. The sample design and per-judge κ script are released with the paper. 

Scope of the calibration sample. We state its limits plainly, since we release it. The sample was drawn from an earlier round of this same pipeline, so its format coverage overlaps but does not coincide with the final eleven-attack suite: it covers four of the five text encoders ( set theory , formal logic , classical language , CodeAttack ) plus a semantic-camouflage encoder not in the final suite, and its image chan-nel uses plain and constant-text renders rather than the six renders of the final suite. It therefore calibrates the judge on the response formats the suite produces — symbolic re-statements, code, and answers to image-delivered requests, including the CodeAttack and image-rendered cases whose grading is most contested — rather than on a per-attack ba-sis. The one final-suite text encoder absent from it, cipher ,carries essentially no signal on these targets ( 0–1% ASR), so little rides on its omission; the render-set difference changes the image stimulus but not the judged artifact, which is al-ways the target’s textual answer. A calibration re-drawn on the final suite would be stronger, and we regard it as the natural next validation step rather than a settled matter. 

Human calibration of the refusal judge. The calibra-tion above validates gpt-5-mini on the harm task. Be-cause over-refusal is the utility axis of our central claim, we calibrate the same judge separately on the refusal task rather than assume the harm result transfers. We drew a second 100 -item blind sample of stored benign responses — 64 representative (stratified by condition × channel over floor /guard-alone/amplifier/ +reguard and text/im-age, balanced answered-vs-refused within each stratum), 30 

enriched from the intrinsically hardest class ( indirect re-fusal ), and 6 topped up — spanning both targets and all five guards, and hand-labeled each against the three-class rubric with the judge’s own verdict withheld. On the binary collapse that the reported metric actually uses (answered vs. refused, over-refusal = direct + indirect), agreement is κ = 0 .794 overall and κ = 0 .875 on the rep-resentative block, with the judge slightly over-flagging ( 8% 

over-flag, 2% miss; 62% judged refusal vs. 56% human). On the full three-class task agreement is weaker, κ = 0 .438 

(0.585 representative), and we report it because its structure 

is informative rather than noisy: the disagreement is almost entirely one-directional along a single boundary — the hu-man assigned direct refusal 35 times against the judge’s 

6, and 29 of the 100 items are human-direct/judge-indirect — and inspection shows these are responses that decline and then offer an alternative, which the rubric assigns to indirect refusal . Both classes count as refusal, so this boundary dis-agreement cannot move the reported quantity; it bears on which kind of refusal a response is, a distinction we never use. We therefore report the binary κ as the figure licens-ing our over-refusal numbers and disclose the three-class figure alongside it. Because a benign prompt recurs across conditions, the sample holds 54 distinct questions among its 

100 responses; resampling one response per distinct ques-tion ( 2000 draws) gives binary κ = 0 .847 [0 .741 , 0.924] —higher, not lower — so the repetition does not inflate agree-ment. 

A transcription artifact in the amplifier’s image path. La-beling surfaced a failure mode worth reporting: on image inputs the amplifier can emit its recover transcription as the final answer (“The image contains the following text: . . . ”), which is not an answer but also not a safety refusal. Across all 5100 scored benign responses we find 26 such echoes and 14 degenerate outputs, and they are confined exactly to where recover runs on images — 3.3% of counted refusals in the amplifier/image cells and 3.0% in +reguard /image, and 0% in every text cell and every cell without the ampli-fier. Counting them as refusals therefore overstates the am-plifier’s image-side over-refusal by roughly two points (e.g. 

60% → 58% ), which changes no conclusion; we leave them counted, and note the bias direction here, because it flatters our own defense to remove them. Our refusal rubric extends OR-Bench’s by one clause classing such an echo as an indi-rect refusal rather than an answer; OR-Bench never sees this format, so upstream has no class for it. 

Judge-panel calibration (on the sample). We scored the same sample with a 26 -judge panel ( 17 rubric-following API models, 9 open guards). The second same-standard judge, gpt-5-nano, reaches κ = 0 .62 . Agreement across the panel splits by standard rather than at random: own-policy guards (WildGuard and peers) flag 60 –70% harmful — close to the human 62% but on their own trained taxonomy — while rubric-following judges are stricter, and the two regimes agree on the non-ambiguous items, contesting only a 9-item core. The practical consequence, which we build into every claim: absolute ASR is judge-dependent, but the direction of every effect is stable across judges. This is why the paper’s conclusions rest on relative contrasts with Wilson CIs and paired McNemar tests (App. P), never on absolute levels. 

Second-judge cross-check (all reported cells). Beyond the validation sample, we re-scored every one of the 449 re-ported harm cells with gpt-5-nano over the identical stored responses (a decoupled rejudge pass, no target re-query). Agreement is very high: per-cell ASR correlates at Pearson 

r = 0 .975 , and the two judges agree on jailbroken-vs-not for 

96% of cells. gpt-5-nano is marginally more lenient (mean per-attack ASR 20 .8% vs. 16 .4% ), exactly as its Round-J calibration predicts. Crucially, every reported contrast re-produces almost exactly. Pooled across guards and both tar-gets, the amplifier reduces per-attack ASR by 51% under 

both judges (guard-alone → amplifier: 21 .9 → 10 .7 under gpt-5-mini, 27 .4 → 13 .5 under gpt-5-nano), and the reguard layer cuts a further 43 –44% (10 .7 → 6.1 and 13 .5 → 7.5

respectively); the gb >mc >+rg ordering holds on both. Abso-lute ASR is modestly judge-dependent, but the relative con-trasts our claims rest on are judge-invariant — the reviewers’ single-judge concern does not affect any conclusion.

# K Extended Related Work 

The main text’s Related Work is condensed for space; this section gives the full treatment. The positioning of our am-plifier against ECSO and the ensemble-evaluation discipline are stated in the main text and repeated here only where the surrounding argument needs them. 

Encoded and cross-modal jailbreaks. A large family of attacks bypasses safety alignment by changing the form of a harmful request rather than its content. Typographic and figure-based renders place the request inside an image (Gong et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2025a; Azulay et al. 2026); distraction attacks disperse it across sub-images or grid cells (Yang et al. 2025); CodeAttack rewrites it as a code-completion task (Ren et al. 2024); and semantic-camouflage attacks embed it in a benign narrative (Yan et al. 2025); and shuffle-inconsistency attacks exploit the gap between a model’s comprehension and its safety response (Zhao et al. 2025b). A compositional variant instead hides the payload in the image embedding 

space, pairing an adversarial image with an innocuous tex-tual prompt so that no readable surface carries the request at all (Shayegani, Dong, and Abu-Ghazaleh 2024). They suc-ceed because a guard trained on natural language does not recognize the encoded surface form — but only the legible-surface family leaves a payload that a recovery step could ever surface, a boundary we make explicit in Limitations. 

Black-box safety guards. Safeguard classifiers screen in-puts and outputs for harmful content: WildGuard (Han et al. 2024), Llama Guard and its vision variant (Inan et al. 2023; Chi et al. 2024), Qwen3Guard (Zhao et al. 2025a), and reasoning-based guards such as ThinkGuard (Wen et al. 2025) and GuardReasoner-VL (Liu et al. 2025b); even purpose-built guards generalize poorly across safety poli-cies (Piao et al. 2026) and their scores can be unstable under prompt rephrasing (Weng, Li, and Martin 2026). They are model-agnostic but inherit the decode gap: they classify what they are shown, and an encoded attack shows them the wrong thing. 

Transform-then-guard defenses. Closest to our work are defenses that transform an input before screening it. ECSO converts image content to text to reactivate text-side safety (Gou et al. 2024); SmoothLLM (Robey et al. 2025) and Se-manticSmooth (Ji et al. 2025) perturb or paraphrase and vote; DoubtProbe verifies a request against a reconstructed struc-tural form (Yin et al. 2026); DefenSee screens sight-side and text-side views of the same input through a multi-view pipeline (Wang, Fok, and Thing 2025); BlueSuffix purifies the input and appends a learned safety suffix rather than screening it (Zhao et al. 2025d); and AdaShield prepends an auto-refined shield prompt against structure-based image attacks (Wang et al. 2024). Our amplifier differs in being explicitly guard-agnostic — it yields a plain-language pay-load any classifier can screen — and in being stress-tested against a cross-modal encoding ensemble rather than single attacks; DoubtProbe, the closest sibling, is not evaluated on cross-modal image-rendered encodings. The technical dis-tinction is a missing capability: ECSO’s transform is a single image-to-caption step — it never inverts an encoding and has no text branch, so symbolic, cipher, or code payloads survive the caption still encoded. Recover-and-decode adds an explicit inverse-transform decode , on text- and image-delivered encodings alike. We make the comparison concrete under our matched protocol: ECSO leaves the eleven-attack ensemble essentially undefended ( 90 /91 % ensemble ASR on Qwen2.5-VL/InternVL3, against an 89/91% undefended floor), because its caption step does not reach the symbolic, cipher, and code encodings in the suite — whereas recover-and-decode cuts the same ensemble to 65 /63 %. Tellingly, ECSO’s per-attack mean ASR is only 25% yet its ensemble is 90% — the very gap our best-of-suite metric exposes. ECSO also pays almost nothing in benign utility for that ( 27% over-refusal on Qwen, against the 26% no-defense floor; Table 1 of the main text), so it occupies the low-cost, low-coverage corner of the same frontier rather than a point off it. 

Broader VLM jailbreak defenses. A parallel line hard-ens the model itself: VLGuard shows safety fine-tuning on a small curated set recovers alignment at almost no ca-pability cost (Zong et al. 2024), SAFEMLLM adversari-ally trains against multimodal jailbreaks (Yin et al. 2025), Robust-VLGuard fine-tunes it for perturbation robustness (Wang et al. 2025a), and AsD overlays a universal pro-tective perturbation synthesized once by white-box opti-mization against a vision encoder (Li, Wang, and Fang 2025). Model-internal methods instead read or steer hid-den representations — tuning-free detection from hidden states (Jiang et al. 2025), representation-shift-based defense (Wei et al. 2026), token-level prune-then-restore (Chen et al. 2025), or representational contrastive scoring (Hua et al. 2026); the same machinery is repurposed to force refusals for image-owner privacy (Shao et al. 2026). All assume white-box access and are complementary to our training-free, guard-agnostic amplifier. Closer to our access model, partial-perception supervision (Zhou et al. 2025a) corrects the target’s answer using its own outputs on cropped sub-images, but targets pixel-perturbation and crop-sensitivity at-tacks rather than the encoded payloads we recover; inference-paradigm choices themselves shift multimodal jailbreak ro-bustness (Tian et al. 2026), and surveys map the wider space (Liu et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2026). A multi-agent alter-native, Agentic Moderation (Ren, Dras, and Naseem 2025), replaces the static guard with cooperating classify/respond/e-valuate/reflect agents — more capable but far heavier than our non-iterative amplifier. Most relevant to our reguard anal-ysis, Long et al. (2025) decompose defenses into safety-shift and harmfulness-discrimination mechanisms and find that stacking two same-mechanism defenses trades helpfulness for safety — a narrower precedent for the cost we measure when stacking a recovery layer and a reguard over an at-tack ensemble. Rigorous and ensemble evaluation. The adversarial-robustness literature has long warned that single-attack eval-uation overstates a defense; reliable evaluation needs an en-semble of diverse attacks (Croce and Hein 2020) and adap-tive, worst-case testing (Carlini et al. 2019; Athalye, Carlini, and Wagner 2018). Surveys of multimodal jailbreaks reach the same conclusion from the defense side, noting that single-attack robustness evaluation “falls short of comprehensively assessing effectiveness” and calling for benchmarks that span diverse attacks (Liu et al. 2024); multimodal benchmarks op-erationalize this joint attack–defense evaluation (Weng et al. 2025; Luo et al. 2024), and dynamic-evaluation suites probe many strategies but report per-strategy degradations rather than the best-of-suite union we adopt (Wang et al. 2026). We further construct our suite so the harmful payload never appears in plain text, avoiding the visual-leakage confound that lets text-only alignment inflate benchmark safety (Hu et al. 2025). We import this discipline to VLM jailbreak de-fense, reporting best-of-suite ASR over standardized harm-ful (Mazeika et al. 2024; Chao et al. 2024) and benign over-refusal (Cui et al. 2025) benchmarks. Certification-style met-rics instead quantify risk analytically — e.g. Retention Score (Li, Chen, and Ho 2025), an ℓ2-bounded robustness certifi-cate — a complement to our empirical best-of-suite success over discrete encoding attacks.

# M Decoder Scale 

Throughout the main results one 7B VLM serves as target, recoverer and decoder, so a natural objection is that the resid-ual measures a weak decoder rather than a limit of recovery-based defense. The amplifier’s two steps separate cleanly on this point: recover is multimodal and needs a VLM, but 

decode is text-only , so the decoder can be replaced indepen-dently while the target still answers and recover still runs on the 7B. We move decode to Llama-3.3-70B ( ∼10 × the pa-rameters) and hold target, recoverer, guards, the eleven-attack suite, the reguard condition, the judge and n=100 fixed, so any change is decoder capacity. A large aligned decoder could simply refuse to restate a harmful prompt, which would return an empty decode, show the guard nothing, and inflate ASR while looking like a clean run. We checked before measuring: on 40 encoded harmful prompts the decoder complied 40 /40 , with no refusals and no empty decodes. # Q Threshold Sweep 

A guard reported at a single operating point cannot distin-guish a mis-calibrated guard from a genuine ceiling. We therefore sweep each guard’s decision threshold τ over its full range and recompute both axes. The sweep is cheap because ASR at τ factorizes: a behavior is broken iff the guard passes it at τ and the target’s answer to it is harm-ful — and the second conjunct does not depend on τ , so it is read from the already-judged undefended floor. Only guard forward passes are recomputed; no target inference and no judging is repeated. Continuous severity comes from the guard’s verdict-token log-probabilities, renormalized over its safe/unsafe vocabulary.
